In 1985 Peter Naur, who had edited the ALGOL 60 report and led one of its first compilers, published a short paper with a large claim: the product of programming is not the program.[^1] It is a *theory*, in Gilbert Ryle's sense of the word: not a written statement but a possession of a person, the kind of knowledge that lets someone act intelligently in a domain and explain what they are doing.[^2] The program text, the documentation, the tests are residue. What actually comes out of building a program is an understanding, held in particular heads, of how some part of the world is being handled by the machine.

Naur gives that understanding three tests. Someone who holds the theory of a program can **relate** it: explain how each part of the program corresponds to the affairs of the world it is meant to handle. They can **justify** it: say why each part is the way it is and not some other way, in terms of the problem rather than the code. And they can **extend** it: take a change request that nobody anticipated and answer it constructively, so that the modified program is still the same kind of thing, not a patch stapled onto a design it contradicts.

The evidence is two stories from his own career. In the first, a group builds a compiler, then hands it to a second group with full documentation, annotated source, and the original authors on call. The second group proposes changes that ignore structure the documentation describes at length; the first group finds the proposals almost impossible to discuss, because the newcomers have not understood the theory and no amount of text will supply it. In the second, a large system is installed for years by people without its theory. The original design stays visible under a decade of "amorphous additions of many different kinds", each one locally reasonable and globally destructive.

From this Naur draws the conclusions people still argue about. A theory cannot be fully written down: it rests on judgements of similarity that, like recognising a face or a tune, cannot be reduced to stated criteria, so documentation is at best a prompt for someone who already has it.[^3] A program is therefore *alive* only while a team that holds its theory exists; when that team dissolves, the program is dead, whether or not it still runs. You discover the death when a change is requested and nobody can answer it intelligently. Revival is possible, but only by a new team building a new theory, and doing so from the text and documentation alone is, in his phrase, "strictly impossible." The honest options are apprenticeship, where the theory passes from person to person, or starting over.

The paper's last section is aimed at what Naur called the production view of programming, the idea that the activity is text production governed by methods, and that programmers are interchangeable executors of them. If the product is a theory, methods cannot substitute for the people who hold it, and a programmer's standing should look less like a factory worker's and more like a professional's, whose value is precisely the understanding that cannot be handed over.

Forty years on, the claim has aged well: the field's own data on how much time goes to reading code rather than writing it, and on how fast a team's knowledge of its own system decays, are exactly what the theory-building view predicts. What has changed is an assumption Naur never had to state, because in 1985 it was true without exception: that you could not obtain a program without someone building its theory first. Part 2 is about what happens now that this is false.
